The part that matters is the third stage

Anyone can have a language model produce a list of companies. It will look excellent and roughly a quarter of the contact details will be invented — plausible addresses assembled from a name pattern rather than read off a page. Send to those and you get bounces, and enough bounces will damage your sending domain for every message after them.

So the pipeline has a verification stage that treats its own output as hostile. Every contact route is confirmed by fetching the page it supposedly appears on and checking the string is really there. Anything that fails is corrected or thrown away.

  1. Define who is actually worth contacting

    Not "SMEs". A specific, testable threshold — for my own offer, whether a company has two to three full-time-equivalent people on one repetitive workflow. Below that line the numbers do not work, and the system is told to reject rather than stretch.

  2. Sweep for it from several angles at once

    Fourteen parallel searches across sectors, regions and trigger types — because any single search angle finds only the companies that angle is good at finding.

  3. Try to disprove every result

    Each candidate goes to a separate check whose job is to refute it: does the company exist, is the contact route genuinely published, is the trigger real and dated, could they plausibly afford this. Failures are dropped, not softened.

  4. Write the opener from the verified trigger

    Not a template with a name merged in. The first line references the specific thing that happened at that company, with the source. If a first line would work for a different company unchanged, it is a bad first line.

What a verified trigger actually looks like

Four real results, described without naming the companies — the names are the part my clients pay for, and those businesses did not volunteer to appear in my marketing. Every one of these came with a dated source that was fetched and confirmed.

17 Aug 2026

A 190-person Nordic accounting group announced an acquisition and, in their own headline, attributed the deal to a technology shift in their industry — while absorbing a second payroll platform alongside the one they already ran.

Why it is a trigger: they have publicly said the problem out loud, and just doubled the integration work that causes it.

27 Aug 2026

National broadcast media reported a specialist glass manufacturer launching a €12M investment programme, led by armoured-vehicle products for the defence sector.

Why it is a trigger: defence supply chains carry a documentation and traceability load that scales with the investment but is rarely budgeted as part of it.

21 May 2026

An electrical contracting group took private-equity money and acquired a competitor in the same announcement. Their contact page names two separate people whose entire job title is quoting.

Why it is a trigger: new capital, a mandate to scale, and two named humans doing the thing that automates best.

A property-management group of 280+ staff, assembled from five acquired brands, advertising for a bookkeeper to do loan-share calculations, VAT returns and purchase ledger by hand.

Why it is a trigger: they are hiring a person to do the exact work the system does. That job advert is the qualification.

What it got wrong

Roughly a quarter of the email addresses the research stage proposed were not on the pages they were attributed to. They were guesses built from a first-name-dot-last-name pattern. One phone number was off by two digits. Four leads could not be confirmed at all and were deleted rather than shipped.

The verification stage also corrected me in the other direction: I flagged one company's number as fabricated, and it turned out to be genuinely published — just on a different page than the one cited. A check that only ever finds faults is a different kind of broken.

That is the honest state of this technology in 2026. It is very good at finding candidates and confidently wrong about details. The value is not in the generating. It is in the machinery that refuses to ship what it cannot prove.
